Trustworthy AI, Fall 2026 — CSCI 6510 / EMSE 6992

Dates:

  • Tuesday, December 8, 2026, 12:00 noon: your paper is due.
  • By Thursday, December 10, 2026: we return your graded paper and your course grade.
  • Wednesday, December 16, 2026, 12:00 noon: the last time to resubmit your paper for a higher grade. This step is optional.

For the final project, you write a research paper about trustworthy AI. You can continue your work from Assignment 1, 2, or 3, or you can start a new topic. If you need a topic, see the project ideas at the end of this page.

Your paper needs a target. A target is a real conference, workshop, or symposium that takes papers. Pick one of the five targets on this page. Then write the kind of paper that your target takes.

You do not have to send your paper to the target, but we encourage it. Most of the targets have deadlines in January or February. You can use our notes from December 10 to improve the paper first.

Groups

Work alone or in a group of up to 3 students. A group submits one plan and one paper.

Join the class Slack workspace to find group members and to ask questions about the project.

Pick a target

Each target has a call for papers. The call is a web page with the rules and the dates. For each target below, the first line tells you what kind of paper fits.

AAAI 2027 Spring Symposia

Pick this target if your paper gives early results or argues for an idea.

  • What it is: AAAI plans about eight small symposia for 2027. Each symposium has one topic and 40 to 75 people. The symposia welcome work in progress and give much time to discussion.
  • What you send: in 2026, most symposia took full papers of 6 to 8 pages. Most also took shorter papers or abstracts of 1 to 4 pages.
  • Dates: AAAI selects the topics by December 8. Then each symposium sets its own paper deadline. In 2026, the deadlines were from January 9 to February 27. The symposia are on March 22–24, 2027, at Santa Clara University in California.
  • Note: you write your paper before you know the topics. If no topic fits your paper, pick a different target. See the 2026 topics for examples. Each presenter must attend in person.

CHI 2027 Posters

Pick this target if your paper is a study of how people use an AI tool.

  • What it is: CHI is the main conference on human-computer interaction. The Posters track takes short studies, case studies, and new ideas. This track now includes the work that CHI called Late-Breaking Work until 2025.
  • What you send: up to 4 pages in the ACM single-column template, plus references. Do not put author names on the paper that you send.
  • Dates: the paper deadline is January 21, 2027. CHI is on May 10–14, 2027, in Pittsburgh.
  • Note: if CHI accepts your paper, one author must register and present a poster. A recorded video is possible if you cannot travel.

ICLR 2027 workshops

Pick this target if your paper has experiments on a model or an agent. Examples are tests of calibration or robustness.

  • What it is: ICLR is a top conference on machine learning. Each workshop is a one-day event on one topic. A workshop paper can show early results.
  • What you send: each workshop sets its own page limit. ICLR also encourages each workshop to take short papers.
  • Dates: ICLR selects the workshops by November 29. Then each workshop sets its own paper deadline. ICLR suggests February 1, 2027. The workshops are on April 29–30, 2027, in San Francisco.
  • Note: pick the specific workshop when the list is available. In 2026, some workshops were about trustworthy AI and about the safety of agents.

ICLR 2027 Blogpost Track

Pick this target only if you can finish a polished blog post about recent machine learning research by December 1.

  • What it is: this track publishes blog posts about recent research from top conferences in machine learning and AI. A post can explain a paper, show its limits, or discuss its effect on society.
  • What you send: a web post in Markdown, not an article. People must write the text. The track can reject a post if an AI wrote the text.
  • Dates: the deadline is December 1, 2026. This is one week before the class deadline for the paper.
  • Note: for the class, submit the URL of your post by December 8.

IJCAI 2027, AI for Social Good track

Pick this target if your project uses AI on a real social problem, with partners who know that problem.

  • What it is: IJCAI is a large international conference on AI. This track wants AI research on problems such as health, education, and the environment. In 2026, the reviewers scored the science, the effect on the real problem, and the work with partners.
  • What you send: a full paper of up to 7 pages, plus up to 2 pages of references. Do not put author names on the paper that you send.
  • Dates: the abstract is due January 4, 2027. The full paper is due January 11, 2027. IJCAI is on August 7–13, 2027, in Kyoto, Japan.
  • Note: this is the hardest target on the list. It needs a complete study, and one author must present in person. IJCAI did not post the 2027 call for this track yet. Read the 2026 call to learn what the track wants.

Read the call of your target before you send a paper. Check the rules for travel, cost, and AI use. We checked the dates on this page on November 9, 2026. Dates can change.

Step 1: The plan, due November 23 (Assignment 4)

Submit a plan for your paper. The plan is one or two pages. Include these items:

  1. The group. Give the names of all group members.
  2. The target. Name your target. Say in one or two sentences why your paper fits it.
  3. The question. Give the main question of your paper in one or two sentences.
  4. The evidence. Say what you have now and what you will add before December 8.
  5. The outline. Give the title of each section and one or two sentences about its content.

AAAI and ICLR did not select their symposia and workshops yet. For these two targets, name only the target in your plan.

Step 2: The paper, due December 8

  • Format: write the paper in the IJCAI format. This is the format of the IJCAI AI for Social Good track. All groups use this format for the class.
  • Template: download the template from the IJCAI Author's Kit page. The kit has LaTeX style files and a Word template.
  • Length: up to 7 pages, plus up to 2 pages of references. This is the IJCAI limit.
  • Names: put the names of all group members on the first page.
  • Exception: for the ICLR Blogpost Track, submit the URL of your post.

If you send your paper to a different target later, move your text into the template of that target.

Project ideas

Here are 8 ideas that are partially filled out. You should probably not do any of these very specifically, they are there to give some ideas at a reasonable level of specificity.

Some ideas name a target that is not in the list of five. These targets are ACL 2027 (language technology), AIES 2027 (AI, ethics, and society), and ICWSM 2027 (web and social media). For these ideas, you can use that target.

1. Every fix has a cost: side effects of prompt rules

Question: When you add a rule to an AI agent's prompt to stop one mistake, how often does the rule cause a new mistake?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with two numbers for each rule. The first is how often the rule fixes the mistake. The second is how often it causes an error in a similar case where it must not apply. A stronger paper shows what moves these numbers, such as the words of the rule.
  • What makes it new: Most studies count how often an agent obeys a rule, or what a rule file costs. Few measure where a rule applies by mistake.
  • Read first: Evaluating AGENTS.md, A Closer Look at System Prompt Robustness, and XSTest, which tests safety behavior in a similar way.
  • Good fit: an ICLR 2027 workshop on agents, or an AAAI Spring Symposium.

2. The quiet guess: requests with two meanings

Question: When a request can mean two things, how often does an AI agent ask, state its choice, or pick one meaning in silence?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with the share of unclear requests where the agent asks, states its choice, or picks one meaning in silence. It also shows how often a silent choice gives the wrong answer. A stronger paper shows that a stated choice helps a reader to catch a wrong guess.
  • What makes it new: Most benchmarks score "ask" against "assume." The stated choice as a third result is new, and so is a test with readers.
  • Read first: Ambig-SWE, LHAW, and Ask Precisely, Assume Wisely.
  • Good fit: CHI 2027 Posters, or ACL 2027 (language technology, papers due January 4).

3. Said and done: what agents report and what they really change

Question: When an AI agent edits files, how often does its final report match the real change?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with two rates for each agent. The first is the share of claimed changes that are really in the file. The second is the share of real changes that the report names. A stronger paper shows when these rates fall, for example on tasks that the agent cannot complete.
  • What makes it new: Studies from 2026 count false claims of success. The reverse is less studied: changes that the agent made but did not report. Office files are a good place to look, because no test checks the work.
  • Read first: From Confident Closing to Silent Failure and Quantifying Overclaiming Propensity in Frontier LLM Agents.
  • Good fit: an ICLR 2027 workshop on agents.

4. Stale but sure: facts that changed this year

Question: For facts that changed in 2026, how often do AI tools give the old fact, and does web search fix it?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with the share of old answers for facts that changed this year, with web search off and on. A stronger paper explains the old answers that remain with search on. The tool did not search, it found only old pages, or it found the new fact and did not use it.
  • What makes it new: Most benchmarks test models and retrieval systems in a lab. The chat tools that people use, with live web search, are less studied.
  • Read first: HoH, StaleBench, and LiveSearchBench.
  • Good fit: IJCAI 2027 (AI for Social Good track), or an ICLR 2027 workshop. For IJCAI, work with a partner who knows local services, such as a library or a city office.

5. Asked about the unwritten: answers that are not online

Question: When the true answer is not on the internet, do AI tools say "I do not know," or do they guess with confidence?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with a comparison of two kinds of questions: answers that are online, and similar answers that are not. It shows how often the tools say "I do not know," and how often they give a confident wrong answer. You must also show that the offline answers are really not online.
  • What makes it new: Benchmarks for local knowledge take their answers from online sources. A pair of questions that differ only in web coverage is new.
  • Take care: Do not include private facts about any person. Do not publish the offline answers. A published answer is online, and then the test does not work.
  • Read first: LocalBench and AbstentionBench.
  • Good fit: AIES 2027 (papers due about May), or IJCAI 2027 (AI for Social Good track).

6. Tell it what you think: how a stated belief moves answers

Question: If a user states a belief first, does the answer of the AI tool move toward the belief, even when the belief is wrong?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with the drop in accuracy when the user states a wrong belief. It also shows the share of right answers that change after one challenge from the user. A stronger paper shows what moves these numbers, such as web search, or a wrong belief that people in a field really hold.
  • What makes it new: Many papers show that a stated belief moves the answer. Two factors are less studied: web search, and how common the wrong belief is in a real field.
  • Read first: When Truth Is Overridden and SPINE.
  • Good fit: CHI 2027 Posters, or an ICLR 2027 workshop.

7. Where was this taken? Location guesses from everyday photos

Question: How close can AI tools place everyday photos, and which simple edits stop them?

  • Endpoint: The paper ends with how often the tools place an everyday photo within a distance that matters, such as 1 kilometer. A stronger paper shows what changes this rate: simple edits such as blur and crop, or a set of photos from one person.
  • What makes it new: Most studies test one photo at a time. Sets of photos from one person are less studied, and so are the simple edits that a phone user can make.
  • Take care: Use only your own photos, or images not used to train models (CC0 imagery). Do not publish the location of any home.
  • Read first: Doxing via the Lens and GEO-Detective.
  • Good fit: ICWSM 2027 (papers due January 15), or AIES 2027 (papers due about May).

8. Sure by design: does a calibrated model know what it does not know?

Question: Is a model trained for calibrated answers still calibrated on statements from your field, and on facts that it cannot know?

  • The model: Jev is a new AI model from TypeSafe. It returns a probability for each yes/no question, not text. Its maker trains it to be calibrated: when it says 90%, it must be right about 90% of the time. You need an API key, and the cost for a class project is small. See the quick start.
  • Endpoint: The paper ends with a comparison, on statements from your field, between the probabilities that Jev states and the share of right answers. A stronger paper finds where this fails. Examples are facts that no model can know, and a statement and its opposite with probabilities that do not add to 1.
  • What makes it new: The first studies test Jev on public benchmarks. Statements from experts that are in no benchmark are new.
  • Take care: Do not use private facts about any person.
  • Read first: Just Ask Jev, Calibration Aware Reinforcement Learning, and Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?
  • Good fit: an ICLR 2027 workshop, or an AAAI Spring Symposium.
