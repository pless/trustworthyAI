For the final project, you write a research paper about trustworthy AI. You can continue your work from Assignment 1, 2, or 3, or you can start a new topic. If you need a topic, see the project ideas at the end of this page.

Your paper needs a target. A target is a real conference, workshop, or symposium that takes papers. Pick one of the five targets on this page. Then write the kind of paper that your target takes.

You do not have to send your paper to the target, but we encourage it. Most of the targets have deadlines in January or February. You can use our notes from December 10 to improve the paper first.

Groups

Join the class Slack workspace to find group members and to ask questions about the project.

Project ideas

Here are seven possible projects. Select an idea to see its plan.

Each plan has a five-sentence version. It shows how the abstract of your paper can read when the work is complete.

Each plan also says what makes the paper new, and it names papers to read first. Your paper must say how it differs from them. The lists are short, so search for newer work before you start.

Two habits make each of these projects stronger. Before any run, write your predictions and the rules for your scores in the plan (Step 1). Then ask a second person to score a sample of the answers, and report how often you agree.

Some ideas name a target that is not in the list of five. These targets are ACL 2027 (language technology), AIES 2027 (AI, ethics, and society), and ICWSM 2027 (web and social media). For these ideas, you can use that target.

1. Every fix has a cost: side effects of prompt rules

Question: When you add a rule to an AI agent's prompt to stop one mistake, how often does the rule cause a new mistake?

Five-sentence version: A common way to fix a mistake of an AI agent is to add a rule to its prompt. We took four tasks from this course where a rule already fixed a known mistake, such as a train route and a campground reservation. For each rule, we wrote target cases, where the rule must apply, and mirror cases, which look the same but need the opposite answer. We ran each case with no rule and with three forms of the rule: strict, limited, and with a reason. We report two rates for each form: how often it fixes the target mistake, and how often it causes an error on a mirror case.

  • Test set: Use four tasks that already have a rule from Assignment 2. For each task, write four target cases and four mirror cases, with the right answers.
  • Mirror cases: A mirror case looks like a target case, but the rule gives the wrong answer there. Example: the rule says that a page that will not load is not evidence that a document does not exist. The mirror case is a document that really does not exist.
  • Rule forms: Write each rule in three forms.
    • Strict: "Never say that a document does not exist."
    • Limited: "If a page will not load, do not say that the document does not exist."
    • With a reason: the limited form, plus why the rule exists.
  • Runs: 4 tasks, 8 cases each, no rule and 3 rule forms, 2 runs each: 256 runs.
  • Score: On target cases, count the mistakes that the rule fixed. On mirror cases, count the new errors. Examples are too much caution, a refusal of a true fact, and a wrong detail that looks checked.
  • Main result: A chart with one point for each rule form. One axis shows the share of target mistakes that the rule fixed. The other axis shows the share of mirror cases with a new error.
  • First step: Take one rule from Assignment 2. Write two mirror cases for it, and run them with and without the rule.
  • What makes it new: Most studies count how often an agent obeys a rule, or what a rule file costs. This study measures where a rule applies by mistake, and how the words of the rule change that.
  • Read first: Evaluating AGENTS.md, A Closer Look at System Prompt Robustness, and XSTest, which uses a similar method for safety behavior.
  • Good fit: an ICLR 2027 workshop on agents, or an AAAI Spring Symposium.

2. The quiet guess: requests with two meanings

Question: When a request can mean two things, how often does an AI agent ask, state its choice, or pick one meaning in silence?

Five-sentence version: Many requests to AI agents can mean two different things, and the agent must pick one. We wrote 40 such requests of four types, plus a clear version of each request as a control. We asked ChatGPT and Claude each request, and marked each answer as asked, stated its choice, or silent choice. Readers with one meaning in mind then judged the answers, and we counted how often they caught an answer for the other meaning. Last, we added one rule, "list the meanings first," and measured the drop in silent choices and the rise in needless questions.

  • Test set: Write 10 requests of each type: a word with two senses, an unstated condition, no unit, and no time or place. For each request, write both meanings, both right answers, and a clear version with only one meaning. Use real documents where you can, such as a job ad or a budget sheet.
  • Runs: 80 requests, 2 tools, without and with the rule: 320 answers.
  • Score: Mark each answer as "asked," "stated its choice," or "silent choice." Then mark if the answer is right for the meaning that it chose. On the clear requests, count the needless questions.
  • Reader test: Give 10 readers 20 answers each: 10 with a silent choice and 10 with a stated choice. Tell the reader which meaning to have in mind. For half of the answers, give the meaning that the tool did not pick. Count how often the reader sees that the answer does not fit.
  • Main result: A table of asked, stated, and silent choices for each type, before and after the rule. A second table shows how often readers caught an answer that did not fit, for silent and for stated choices.
  • First step: Collect five real unclear requests from your earlier assignments.
  • What makes it new: Most benchmarks score "ask" against "assume." This study scores the stated choice as a third result, and it tests if a stated choice helps the reader.
  • Read first: Ambig-SWE, LHAW, and Ask Precisely, Assume Wisely.
  • Good fit: CHI 2027 Posters, or ACL 2027 (language technology, papers due January 4).

3. Said and done: what agents report and what they really change

Question: When an AI agent edits files, how often does its final report match the real change?

Five-sentence version: AI agents now edit files for people, and they end each task with a report of what they changed. We built 30 small office tasks with a known result, such as an update to a project tracker from an email. Ten of the tasks are blocked: for example, the instruction names a row that does not exist. We ran two agents three times on each task, and a short script compared each claimed change with the real file. We count changes that the agent claimed but did not make, and changes that it made but did not report, for clear and blocked tasks.

  • Test set: Make 30 tasks: 20 clear tasks and 10 blocked tasks. For each task, save the start file, the instruction, and the correct end file.
  • Blocked tasks: A blocked task has no full solution. For example, the instruction names a row that does not exist. A good report says what the agent could not do.
  • Small errors: In some start files, put a small error near the target, such as a typo. Do not ask the agent to fix it. Then you can see changes that nobody asked for.
  • Tools: Use two agents that can edit files, such as Codex and Claude Code. Use copies of files, never real data.
  • Runs: 30 tasks, 2 agents, 3 runs each: 180 runs.
  • Check: Write a small script that compares the end file with the start file and with the correct file, cell by cell. List the claims in each report by hand.
  • Score: Report two rates. The first rate is the share of claimed changes that are really in the file. The second rate is the share of real changes that the report names.
  • Main result: A table of the two rates for each agent, for clear tasks and for blocked tasks.
  • First step: Make two clear tasks and one blocked task, and run each one once on each agent.
  • What makes it new: Studies from 2026 count false claims of success. This study also counts the reverse: changes that the agent made but did not report. It uses office files, where no test checks the work.
  • Read first: From Confident Closing to Silent Failure and Quantifying Overclaiming Propensity in Frontier LLM Agents.
  • Good fit: an ICLR 2027 workshop on agents.

4. Stale but sure: facts that changed this year

Question: For facts that changed in 2026, how often do AI tools give the old fact, and does web search fix it?

Five-sentence version: Many facts change, but AI tools learn from older text. We collected 60 facts that changed in 2026, such as a race that moved to a new city, each with an official source and date. We asked three AI tools about each fact with web search off and on, plus 20 facts that did not change. For each old answer with search on, we found the cause: no search, only old pages found, or the new fact found but not used. We show how the share of old answers falls with the weeks since the change, and which areas stay wrong the longest.

  • Test set: Find 15 changed facts in each of four areas: travel, law and policy, sports and events, and local businesses or services. Record the date of each change and the source. Also pick 20 facts of the same kinds that did not change.
  • Question form: Ask for the current value. Do not hint that it changed.
  • Runs: 80 facts, 3 tools, search off and on: 480 answers.
  • Cause: With search on, save the pages that each tool cites. Give each old answer one of three causes: no search, only old pages found, or the new fact found but not used.
  • Main result: A chart of the share of old answers against the weeks since the change, for each area. A table of the three causes for each tool.
  • First step: Find 10 changed facts in your own field, and check that each has an official date.
  • What makes it new: Most benchmarks test models and retrieval systems in a lab. This study tests the chat tools that people use, with live web search. It also finds the step where the old answer comes from.
  • Read first: HoH, StaleBench, and LiveSearchBench.
  • Good fit: IJCAI 2027 (AI for Social Good track), or an ICLR 2027 workshop. For IJCAI, work with a partner who knows local services, such as a library or a city office.

5. Asked about the unwritten: answers that are not online

Question: When the true answer is not on the internet, do AI tools say "I do not know," or do they guess with confidence?

Five-sentence version: Some facts that matter to people are not on the internet, such as how a town pays for transport during a cash shortage. We wrote 30 questions whose answers we know but no website gives, each with a matched question whose answer is online. We asked three AI tools each question two times with web search on, and a second person checked our answer key. We measure how often the tools say "I do not know," how often they guess, and if the pages that they cite support the guess. The gap shows a risk for places with little online coverage, where a confident guess can look like a fact.

  • Test set: Write 30 questions that only people in your community or workplace can answer. Match each one with a question about the same place and the same kind of fact. The answer to the matched question must be online. Also write 10 questions about events that never happened, as a trap.
  • Proof: Show that each offline answer is not online. Search the web with a fixed list of queries, and save the queries and the date. Remove a question if you find its answer.
  • Check: Ask a second person who knows the topic to confirm each answer. Do not include private facts about any person.
  • Runs: 70 questions, 3 tools, 2 runs: 420 answers.
  • Score: Mark each answer as right, "I do not know," or wrong. For each wrong answer, mark if the tool cites pages, and if those pages support the answer.
  • Main result: A table of right answers, "I do not know" answers, and confident wrong answers, for each kind of question. Show the wrong answers with and without cited pages.
  • Release: Do not publish the offline answers. A published answer is online, and then the test does not work. Publish the method and the online questions.
  • First step: Write five offline questions and five matched online questions, and test them once.
  • What makes it new: Benchmarks for local knowledge take their answers from online sources. In this study, the two questions of a pair differ in one thing only: the answer is online or it is not.
  • Read first: LocalBench and AbstentionBench.
  • Good fit: AIES 2027 (papers due about May), or IJCAI 2027 (AI for Social Good track). A community partner that can run the test again makes the paper stronger.

6. Tell it what you think: how a stated belief moves answers

Question: If a user states a belief first, does the answer of the AI tool move toward the belief, even when the belief is wrong?

Five-sentence version: AI tools may agree with what a user already believes, even when the belief is wrong. We wrote 40 questions in our own fields, each with a wrong belief that people in the field really hold and an unlikely wrong belief. We asked two AI tools the plain question and both belief forms in new chats, with web search off and on. After each right answer we challenged the tool once, and we recorded if it searched the web before its reply. We measure how much each kind of wrong belief lowers accuracy, and if web search helps a tool to hold a right answer.

  • Test set: Write 40 questions with known answers. For each question, find a wrong belief that people in your field really hold, with a source that shows it. Also write an unlikely wrong belief. Then write three forms of the question: plain, with the common wrong belief, and with the unlikely wrong belief.
  • Challenge: After each right answer, write "I read the opposite." Then record the new answer, and record if the tool ran a search.
  • Runs: 40 questions, 3 forms, 2 tools, search off and on: 480 answers, plus the challenges.
  • Main result: A table of accuracy for the three forms, with search off and on. A second table shows the share of right answers that changed after one challenge.
  • First step: Write 10 questions in the three forms, and run them on one tool.
  • What makes it new: Many papers show that a stated belief moves the answer. This study adds two factors: web search, and how common the wrong belief is in a real field.
  • Read first: When Truth Is Overridden and SPINE.
  • Good fit: CHI 2027 Posters, or an ICLR 2027 workshop.

7. Where was this taken? Location guesses from everyday photos

Question: How close can AI tools place everyday photos, and which simple edits stop them?

Five-sentence version: AI tools can guess where someone took a photo, which is a privacy risk for people who share photos online. We used 100 of our own photos with known locations, and removed the location data from each file before each test. We asked three AI tools for the location of single photos, and of sets of 3, 5, and 10 photos from one neighborhood. We repeated the test after simple edits that blur text or crop the background, and rated how well each edited photo still showed its subject. We measure how often each tool puts the camera within 100 meters, 1 kilometer, and 25 kilometers, and how sets and edits change these rates.

  • Test set: Pick 25 of your own photos of each of four types: famous places, streets with signs, plain streets or parks, and rooms.
  • Sets: Also make 6 sets of 10 photos. All photos of a set are from one neighborhood, and none shows a famous place. Ask each tool for the neighborhood with 1, 3, 5, and 10 photos of the set.
  • Edits: For each photo that a tool placed within 1 kilometer, make three edited copies: text blurred, background cropped, and both. Rate each copy from 1 to 5 for how well it still shows its subject.
  • Check: Use the location data in the original file as the true answer, and compute the distance for each guess. For a set, use the center of its photo locations.
  • Main result: A chart of the share of photos within each distance, by type of photo and by edit. A second chart shows how the distance error falls when the set grows.
  • First step: Test 10 photos on one tool to check the prompt and the distance script.
  • Ethics: Use only your own photos, or images not used to train models (CC0 imagery). Do not publish the location of any home. Make the center of each set a public place.
  • What makes it new: Most studies test one photo at a time. This study tests sets of photos from one person. It also tests the simple edits that a phone user can make, and what each edit costs.
  • Read first: Doxing via the Lens and GEO-Detective.
  • Good fit: ICWSM 2027 (papers due January 15), or AIES 2027 (papers due about May).
